What is an ACL injury?

An ACL injury refers to any sprain or tear of the anterior cruciate ligament — a rope-like band of tissue that sits inside the knee joint and connects the thigh bone (femur) to the shin bone (tibia). The ACL is one of the primary stabilisers of the knee, controlling forward movement of the tibia and helping the knee resist twisting forces.

Ligament injuries are graded on severity. A Grade 1 injury is a mild sprain, where the fibres are stretched but the ligament remains intact. A Grade 2 injury is a partial tear. A Grade 3 injury is a complete rupture, in which the ligament is torn all the way through and can no longer stabilise the joint on its own.

ACL injuries are among the most common serious knee injuries seen in orthopaedic clinics in India, particularly in patients who play cricket, football, badminton, kabaddi or basketball, and in dancers or gym-goers who load the knee under rotation.

Anatomy of the knee ligaments

The knee joint is stabilised by four principal ligaments working together. Understanding where they sit makes it easier to picture what happens during an injury.

  • Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) — sits deep inside the knee joint, crossing diagonally, and prevents the tibia from sliding forward on the femur.
  • Posterior Cruciate Ligament (PCL) — pairs with the ACL, controlling backward movement of the tibia.
  • Medial Collateral Ligament (MCL) — runs along the inner side of the knee.
  • Lateral Collateral Ligament (LCL) — runs along the outer side of the knee.

Alongside these ligaments, the menisci — two crescent-shaped pads of cartilage — act as shock absorbers between the thigh bone and shin bone. Because the ACL and the menisci sit so close together, an ACL injury sometimes damages the meniscus at the same time.

Symptoms of an ACL tear

ACL injuries usually come on suddenly, at the moment of the injury. Common signs and symptoms include:

  • A distinct "pop" heard or felt in the knee at the time of injury.
  • Rapid swelling around the knee within a few hours, caused by bleeding inside the joint.
  • Pain, particularly with weight-bearing and when trying to straighten the knee.
  • A feeling of instability — the knee "giving way" when you try to change direction.
  • Reduced range of motion — the knee feels stiff and difficult to bend fully.
  • Tenderness along the joint line.
  • Difficulty walking normally, particularly on stairs or uneven surfaces.

If any of these symptoms follow a twisting injury or an awkward landing, it is worth having the knee examined promptly rather than waiting for the swelling to settle on its own.

Common causes

ACL injuries can be non-contact or contact-based. The classic mechanisms include:

  • Sudden pivots or changes in direction — turning quickly with the foot planted, common in football, cricket fielding and badminton.
  • Awkward landings — landing from a jump with the knee slightly bent inward, as often seen in basketball and volleyball.
  • Sudden deceleration — slowing down abruptly while running.
  • Direct impact to the knee — a tackle, collision or fall that forces the knee into an unnatural position.
  • Missteps and falls — slipping on uneven ground or missing a step on stairs.
  • Road traffic accidents — high-energy trauma that can involve multiple ligaments.

Certain factors — such as muscle imbalances, previous knee injuries, footwear and the surface you play on — can also raise the risk of an ACL injury over time.

How ACL injuries are diagnosed

Diagnosis usually starts with a clinical examination by an orthopaedic doctor or sports physician. They will ask about the mechanism of injury, whether you heard a pop, how quickly the knee swelled, and how stable the knee feels now.

Special manual tests such as the Lachman test, the anterior drawer test and the pivot-shift test are commonly used to check how much the tibia moves forward on the femur. These give the examining doctor a strong indication of whether the ACL is likely to be torn.

Imaging is typically used to confirm the diagnosis and to look for associated injuries. An X-ray helps rule out fractures, while an MRI scan is the standard test for visualising ligaments and menisci in detail. Your treating team will interpret the images alongside the clinical findings before recommending a management plan.

This page is educational and does not replace an in-person assessment — please consult your orthopaedic surgeon or a qualified physiotherapist for a personalised diagnosis.

How functional bracing helps ACL recovery

Functional knee braces are designed to support the knee after an ACL injury, both for patients managing the injury without surgery and for those recovering after ACL reconstruction. They typically use a rigid frame with hinges and straps to control how far the tibia can move forward on the femur — mimicking, in part, what a healthy ACL would do.

Bracing does not heal the ligament, but it can meaningfully change how the knee feels during activity. Many patients describe a step-up in confidence when the knee no longer feels like it is about to "give way", particularly on stairs, uneven ground and during pivoting movements.

In post-surgical recovery, your surgeon may prescribe a functional brace once you are past the initial rehabilitation phase, to protect the healing graft as you return to sport. In non-surgical management, a brace can be part of a broader plan alongside targeted physiotherapy and gradual loading. Established international bracing systems — including Breg functional braces distributed in India by our team — are commonly used across both pathways.

The right brace depends on your anatomy, activity level and the specific instructions of your treating doctor. Always follow their guidance on when to wear the brace, and for how long.

ACL recovery timeline

Recovery from an ACL injury is a gradual, phased process. The exact milestones depend on whether you have surgery, the type of graft used, your baseline fitness, and the intensity of the activities you want to return to. The outline below is a general reference — your surgeon or physiotherapist will personalise it for you.

  1. Weeks 0–2 — Immediate care

    The priority is to bring the swelling down, protect the knee and start restoring basic range of motion. Ice, elevation, gentle motion exercises and a knee brace or immobiliser (if prescribed) are typical during this phase.

  2. Weeks 2–6 — Early rehabilitation

    Physiotherapy focuses on regaining full knee extension, walking normally without crutches, and reactivating the quadriceps muscles. Static and light closed-chain exercises are usually introduced.

  3. Months 2–4 — Functional rehabilitation

    Strength work steps up. Squats, step-ups, cycling and controlled leg-press variations help rebuild leg strength and balance. Many patients are cleared for jogging in a straight line by the end of this phase, subject to their surgeon's assessment.

  4. Months 4–9+ — Return to sport

    Sport-specific drills — cutting, pivoting, jumping and landing — are gradually reintroduced under the supervision of a physiotherapist. Return-to-play is typically cleared only after strength, hop testing and neuromuscular control meet defined criteria, not simply after a set number of months.

Note: Individual timelines vary widely. Always follow the specific rehabilitation protocol given by your orthopaedic surgeon and physiotherapist — do not use this timeline to self-progress your rehab.

Frequently asked questions

Do I always need surgery for a torn ACL?

Not always. Whether surgery is recommended depends on the grade of the tear, your age, activity goals and how unstable the knee feels day to day. Many partial tears and low-demand lifestyles are managed successfully with structured physiotherapy and a functional brace. Your orthopaedic surgeon is best placed to advise on the right pathway for you.

How long does an ACL injury take to heal?

Recovery varies significantly. A mild sprain may settle in a few weeks with rest and rehab, while a complete tear that is surgically reconstructed typically takes six to nine months before a full return to sport. Milestones are set by your surgeon and physiotherapist, not by a fixed calendar.

Can I still walk with a torn ACL?

Many people can walk on a torn ACL, especially once the initial swelling settles, but the knee may feel unstable on uneven ground, stairs or during pivoting movements. A functional brace can help provide short-term stability, but walking without pain is not the same as a healed knee — please consult your doctor.

What is the difference between a knee sprain and a tear?

A sprain describes stretched or partially damaged ligament fibres, graded from mild (Grade 1) to severe. A complete tear (Grade 3) means the ligament has ruptured. Grading is confirmed by clinical examination and imaging such as an MRI scan.

Which brace is best for ACL support?

Functional ACL braces are the category typically prescribed after an ACL injury or reconstruction. The specific model depends on your anatomy, activity level and your surgeon's preference. Our clinical team can help you understand the options once we know your case history.

Can I return to sports after an ACL tear?

Yes, many people return to their previous level of sport after appropriate rehabilitation. Return-to-play decisions are based on strength, single-leg control, hop testing and clinical confidence — usually cleared by your surgeon and physiotherapist together.

Are functional braces available on prescription in India?

Yes. Functional knee braces from international brands are stocked and shipped pan-India through our authorised retail partner, and are typically dispensed based on an orthopaedic prescription and a proper size fitting.

Does the brace hurt to wear?

A correctly sized functional brace should feel supportive rather than painful. Some initial pressure or awareness is normal in the first few days, but persistent pinching, chafing or numbness usually means the brace needs to be refitted — please consult your fitter or physiotherapist.
